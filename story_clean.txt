The first time I tasted real Java coffee, it wasn't in a caf .

It was on the island of Java, before sunrise, when the air still felt cool and damp and the world hadn't started shouting yet.

Indonesia wakes up early.

From the ridge above the plantation, you could see the faint outline of volcanoes in the distance - quiet giants that built the soil one eruption at a time. The farmers said that's why the coffee tastes the way it does. Volcanic earth. Deep minerals. Slow growth. Patience burned into every bean.

Indonesia isn't just one place. It's thousands. Over 17,000 islands stitched together by water and trade winds, forming the country we call Indonesia. But Java holds a special reputation. In fact, for centuries, "a cup of java" didn't just mean coffee from the island. It meant coffee, period.

The Dutch figured that out early.

Back in the 1600s, when the Dutch East India Company decided spices weren't enough, they brought coffee plants from Yemen and planted them in Java's highlands. The climate cooperated. The soil cooperated. The farmers adapted. And soon, ships were leaving Batavia loaded with dark green beans that would change breakfast forever.

That morning, I watched Pak Arif - third-generation grower - walk between rows of coffee trees, checking leaves with the precision of someone who understands that small details decide everything. He handed me a single red cherry.

"Try," he said.

I bit into it. Sweet. Almost like fruit candy. Not bitter at all.

"Coffee is born sweet," he added. "We make it bitter."

Later, under a tin roof that rattled when the wind picked up, the beans were drying on raised beds. A few meters away, his wife roasted a small batch in a drum over flame. The smell wasn't like the sharp, burnt scent from supermarket coffee. It was thicker. Warmer. Like chocolate and smoke arguing politely.

When she poured the brew, it was thick and bold but strangely smooth. Earthy. A little spicy. Low acidity. The kind of coffee that doesn't punch you - it anchors you.

That's the thing about Java coffee. It doesn't rush.

Indonesia doesn't either.

Scooters hum past mosques at prayer time. Markets fill with durian and chilies. Rain comes fast and leaves just as quickly. And in the middle of it all, coffee ties people together - on plastic stools in alleyways, in roadside warungs, in highland farms where clouds drift low enough to touch.

In the city, someone orders an espresso and calls it "java" without thinking about it. On the island, that word still has weight. It carries history, colonization, resilience, and quiet craftsmanship.

As the sun climbed and the volcano peaks sharpened against the sky, Pak Arif poured a second cup.

"People drink coffee to wake up," he said.

He smiled.

"But here, it reminds us where we are."

And that might be the real flavor of Java - not just boldness or earthiness, but identity. Grown in volcanic soil. Shipped across oceans. Poured into cups around the world.

Yet still rooted in Indonesia's red earth.

# Red Dust and Rising Sun

The drought had cracked the red earth of the Darling Downs into a mosaic of parched clay, and old Bill Hargreaves said it looked like the world itself was splitting apart. He wasn't wrong. It was 1942, and the world *was* splitting apart  -  only the fault lines ran through places with names no Australian farmer had ever had reason to learn. Guadalcanal. Tobruk. El Alamein.

The kangaroos didn't know any of this, of course. They knew the smell of eucalyptus and the particular silence that falls over the scrub before a storm. They knew the water holes that remained when the creek beds went dry and the tender shoots that pushed up through the spinifex after rain. Mob life was governed by older rhythms than war.

But war came to them anyway.

---

At the RAAF base outside Townsville, a young ground mechanic from Geelong named Tommy Vickers had taken to leaving scraps outside the perimeter fence. It started with a single grey female  -  enormous ears swivelling like radar dishes  -  who appeared one evening at dusk with a joey peeking from her pouch. Tommy called her Margaret, after his mother. By the end of his first month in Queensland he had a modest congregation of seven.

The other men laughed at him, then one by one they started coming out to watch. There was something about the kangaroos that steadied them. You could talk while you watched them, and somehow the talking came easier with something else to look at. A mechanic from Ballarat named Ernie confessed he was terrified of flying  -  not of dying, precisely, but of the moment before. A pilot called Clancy, who wore his confidence like a flight suit, admitted quietly that he'd stopped writing letters home because he couldn't think of anything true to say that he was also allowed to say.

Margaret would watch them with those amber, long-lashed eyes and chew in her patient, lateral way, and nobody felt judged.

---

When the Japanese bombers came in on the nineteenth of February and struck Darwin, the sound of distant war became suddenly less distant. Bases across northern Australia went on high alert. The kangaroos around the Townsville perimeter scattered into the scrub for two days, spooked by the change in human energy  -  the running boots, the aircraft engines running at all hours, the shouting.

But Margaret came back. She came back because Tommy came back to the fence, even on the nights when he should have slept, sitting in the dark with his back against a gum post and talking quietly to no one in particular. Partly to Margaret. Partly to himself. Partly to God, though he would never have admitted the last part.

He talked about the mechanics of Kittyhawk engines. He talked about a girl in Geelong called Susan who had cut her hair short and was now working in a munitions factory and seemed, from her letters, to be having a rather better war than he was. He talked about his father, who had come back from the last war with all his limbs but something missing in his eyes, some essential light that Tommy had never quite been able to name or reach.

Margaret ate her scraps and blinked and chewed.

---

In the Western Australian outback, far from any base, the kangaroos went about their lives with magnificent indifference. Mobs of reds moved across the ancient landscape in the blue hour before sunrise, their shadows long and strange on the ochre ground. An old boomer  -  massive through the chest, his forearms scarred from a dozen territorial disputes  -  led his group to a waterhole that had been used by his kind since before white men arrived, before the Noongar people named the stars, perhaps before human memory altogether.

The waterhole reflected the sky, and the sky that year was sometimes crossed by aircraft  -  shapes that had no precedent in any kangaroo's inherited understanding of the world. The old boomer would watch them with the same attentive caution he gave to wedge-tailed eagles: potential threat, source unclear, best to be ready.

Then the shapes would pass, and the waterhole would be still again, and the sky would return to being simply the sky.

---

Tommy Vickers survived the war. He went home to Geelong in 1945 a quieter man than he'd left, as men tended to do, but not hollowed out the way his father had been. He married Susan  -  she'd kept her hair short, and he'd liked it that way  -  and they had three children and eventually a small hardware business that did well enough.

He never forgot the kangaroos. On still evenings, sitting on the back verandah with a cup of tea going cold, he'd sometimes think about Margaret and her joey and the way the mob had moved in the last of the Queensland light. He thought about how they'd kept their own lives running  -  feeding, resting, raising young, following the old watercourses  -  while the human world burned and remade itself around them.

It hadn't been indifference, he decided. It was something harder to name. Persistence, maybe. The simple, stubborn continuation of life.

He found it comforting, still.

# The Children of Ra at the Edge of the World

The reed boats had been at sea for forty-one days when Amenhotep's navigator finally saw birds  -  not the ocean birds they had grown accustomed to, but land birds, small and green and screaming at each other in a language no one aboard could name.

"There," said the navigator, a lean man from Alexandria named Yusuf, pointing southwest into the haze. He had been born skeptical of everything except the stars, and the stars had not failed him.

Amenhotep stood at the prow and watched the horizon thicken into something solid. He was not a young man. He had crossed the great desert twice, had traded in Nubia and sailed the Red Sea to the spice kingdoms of the East. But this  -  this had been a madman's commission, born out of a conversation with a Phoenician sailor who had returned from an impossible voyage and spoken, drunk and trembling, of a land where the sun set on mountains made of gold.

The fleet was twelve vessels, none of them large. They had followed the trade winds south along the African coast, then caught the great western current that swept them across open ocean with terrifying indifference. Three boats had been lost. Sixty men. Their names had been spoken into the water each morning since.

---

The coast they found was rocky, indifferent to them, vast. They sailed south for two more weeks before the cliffs broke apart into a gentler shore, and they went in.

The people who came down to the beach were copper-skinned and sharp-eyed, armed with slings and wooden clubs and an absolute absence of fear that Amenhotep found immediately respectable. He had expected to be met with terror. He was not. The men on the beach watched the strange boats approach with something closer to curiosity  -  and, behind that, the patient readiness of people who have defended their land before and are quite prepared to do it again.

Amenhotep ordered his men to stay at the oars. He stepped off the boat alone, holding nothing in his hands.

There was a long silence on the beach. The sun was sharp and thin at this altitude  -  he had not expected altitude, had not known the land rose so steeply so quickly behind the coast. His ears had ached for days as they'd climbed the river valley.

A woman walked forward from the gathered crowd. She was perhaps fifty, wrapped in cloth of extraordinary intricacy  -  patterns that seemed to move when you looked away from them. She had the bearing of someone who issued commands and was not accustomed to repeating them.

She looked at Amenhotep the way an architect looks at a building she is deciding whether to demolish or restore.

She said something. He did not understand a word of it.

He reached into the satchel at his side and removed a small faience figurine  -  Thoth, the god of knowledge and translation  -  and held it out to her. Not as an offering. As an introduction.

She studied it. She took it. She turned it over in her fingers, examined the blue glaze, the ibis head, the careful proportions. Then she looked up at him with an expression that, across any distance of language or culture, translated perfectly:

*Well. You are not nothing, are you.*

---

They stayed.

Not by conquest  -  Amenhotep had brought sixty men, not six thousand, and the people of this high kingdom were not the kind anyone conquered easily, as several ambitious neighbors had already discovered at great cost. They stayed the way a new word stays: absorbed, argued over, changed by the mouth that speaks it.

The Egyptians had papyrus. The Andeans had fiber technologies that made Egyptian linen look like burlap. The Egyptians had a writing system. The Andeans had the quipu  -  knots that held numbers, histories, laws  -  and several of the scribes in Amenhotep's party became obsessed with it, filling their notebooks with sketches, waking in the middle of the night with new theories about what a particular color of cord might mean.

The Andeans had potatoes, and maize, and quinoa, and guinea pigs, and the llama  -  an animal so magnificently unbothered by human pretension that the Egyptians fell in love with them immediately and were disappointed to learn they could not be ridden.

The Egyptians had bread-making traditions, astronomical knowledge, and a surplus of opinions about architecture that the local stonemasons found intermittently useful and mostly irritating, since the Andean approach to fitting stone was already, quietly, one of the great engineering achievements in the history of the world.

---

Amenhotep died in the highlands, nine years after landfall, having learned enough of the language to argue theology in it, which he did constantly and with great pleasure. He was buried in the Andean fashion, seated, wrapped in fine cloth, his faience figurines arranged around him  -  Thoth, and Ra, and small green-glazed Osiris  -  alongside offerings of maize and dried potato and a quipu whose knots, the woman who had first met him on the beach insisted on tying herself.

No one recorded what she knotted into it.

Some said it was his name. Some said it was a history of the crossing  -  the forty-one days, the three lost boats, the sixty names spoken into the ocean. Some said it was a question, and that she had never received an answer she found satisfying, and that the question would wait in the knots until someone came along who could read it properly.

The fleet never returned to Egypt. The story of the voyage survived in fragments, in a footnote in a document no one could authenticate, in a minor character in a story about something else entirely.

The quipu, if it survived, has not been found.

The question waits.

# The Parting of the Red Sea (Prehistoric Edition)

The sand shook beneath Moses' sandals, but not from the footsteps of Pharaoh's cavalry.

No  -  it was the *other* problem.

"Moses!" cried Miriam, sprinting up beside him, her robes whipping in the desert wind. "The Egyptians are behind us, the sea is in front of us, and there is a *Tyrannosaurus rex* eating one of the supply carts!"

Moses turned. Sure enough, the massive creature had the cart clamped in its jaws, shaking it like a dog with a sandal. Behind it, a whole herd of Triceratops thundered across the dunes, their horns gleaming in the afternoon sun. Further back, the unmistakable silhouette of a Spinosaurus waded out of the Nile, apparently having followed them the whole way from Egypt.

No one had mentioned the dinosaurs in any of the prophecies. Moses made a mental note to bring this up later.

"Keep moving!" he bellowed to the two million Israelites who had ground to a bewildered halt  -  half of them staring at the sea, half of them staring at the prehistoric reptiles, all of them deeply regretting leaving Egypt.

"I knew we should have stayed," muttered a man named Gerald. Several people nodded.

Moses strode to the water's edge and raised his staff. He wasn't entirely sure what he was doing  -  he never entirely was  -  but faith had carried him this far, through plagues and Pharaohs and a burning bush that somehow hadn't burned. Surely it could handle a few terrible lizards.

The wind came first. A great, impossible roar of it, warm and ancient and smelling of something beyond the desert, beyond the sea, beyond anything Moses could name. The water began to tremble. Then it began to *move.*

The Red Sea split.

Two walls of water rose on either side, green and shimmering, fish suspended inside them like decorations in glass. Seaweed hung in midair. A bewildered crab, finding itself suddenly exposed, made a break for dry land.

A path stretched out before them  -  wide, flat, and dry as the desert they'd crossed.

The Israelites stared.

"WALK!" Moses roared.

They walked.

Behind them, the T. rex paused at the water's edge, tiny arms raised in what might have been confusion or fury  -  it was hard to tell with a T. rex. It let out a bellow that echoed off the walls of water and sent every bird in a ten-mile radius into panicked flight.

Then, because it was a T. rex and had roughly the decision-making skills of a large boulder, it charged in after them.

So did the Triceratops. And the Spinosaurus. And, unfortunately, what turned out to be a very large pack of Velociraptors who had been cleverly concealed behind a dune the entire time, waiting for exactly this kind of chaotic opportunity.

The Israelites, to their credit, did not stop moving. Years of slavery had taught them to keep their heads down and their feet going. They streamed through that impossible corridor of water, children on shoulders, elders carried in arms, goats bleating, camels deeply unimpressed.

Moses was the last one through.

He stood at the far shore and turned. The dinosaurs were halfway across now  -  the T. rex in the lead, eyes fixed on him with that particular expression of a predator who has decided things are about to go its way.

Moses lowered his staff.

The walls came down.

The sound was like the world ending and beginning at the same time. A mountain of water collapsed inward, swallowing the roar of the T. rex mid-bellow. The sea churned white and furious, then, slowly, settled.

The strait was whole again. Calm. Glittering in the late sun as if nothing had happened at all.

Silence fell over the shore. Two million people stood dripping with sea spray, hearts hammering, staring at the water.

"Right," said Moses, turning toward the wilderness ahead. "Forty years. Let's get moving."

"Did that just - " Miriam began.

"Yes."

"Were those - "

"Also yes."

She looked at the sea one more time, then back at her brother. "I'm going to write a song about this."

"You do that," said Moses, and walked on into the desert, staff in hand, utterly certain of only one thing:

No one was *ever* going to believe this.

ESP8266 Weather Logger

One of my favorite projects combines an ESP8266, a DHT11 sensor, and a few LEDs to log and display temperature and humidity. An esp8266 is a small and cheap development board similar to what an arduino board is.


An esp8266 board slotted into a header pin adapter
Basically, the difference between arduino and espressive (esp8266 and esp32) is the price and physical size. That and the espressive boards have onboard wifi and bluetooth.

The reason this project was so much fun was partially because I programmed a Telegram bot into the esp8266 board. Do you know what this means? I can message it to get the current temperature! It also can return a csv spreadsheet file that it stores on its admittedly very tiny flash storage. I believe it can store up to 2 months of logging the temperature and humidity every hour. I'd like to make it upload this data to google sheets whenever it ran out of storage and then clear the storage for more logging.

Additionally I added indicators for temperature thresholds: a blue and a red LED. They turn on when the temperature rises or falls on 20 C.)
